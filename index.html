The game

Two colonels each split S soldiers across n castles worth v1…vn points (here castle i is worth i). Both choose at the same time and in secret. On each castle, more soldiers wins. A tie scores for nobody (or splits, if you pick that). Whoever has more points wins the match.

Classic:   scoreA = Σi : ai > bi vi

The Jane Street penalty variant

Overkill costs you. On every castle you win, you lose λ = 0.2 points for each soldier you have above your opponent’s count:

Penalty:   scoreA = Σi : ai > bi ( vi − λ · (ai − bi) )

Read literally, even the winning margin of 1 costs 0.2. Some versions charge only for soldiers beyond the one you needed, i.e. (a − b − 1). Both are in the rules bar. Either way, a win by 40 soldiers on castle 9 is worth 9 − 8 = 1 point instead of 9. Stacking soldiers on a castle you already hold is now actively harmful, so good strategies spread out and try to win by small margins.

Why no allocation is “the best”

Every pure allocation can be beaten. If B shows you their plan, you can take back one soldier from some castles and add one where it flips a loss into a win. Tab 1’s Best reply button does exactly that. So the game has no pure-strategy equilibrium. Its equilibria are mixed: probability distributions over allocations. Borel (1921) and Roberson (2006) solved the continuous, equal-value case. The discrete, unequal-value case here has no closed form, which is why we simulate.

Best reply by dynamic programming

Against a known opponent, or a known bag of opponents, each castle’s expected point differential depends only on how many soldiers you put there:

fi(x) = (1/m) Σj=1..m diff(x, bj,i, vi),   diff = +(my points) if x > b;  −(their points) if x < b;  0 if tie

We want to maximise Σ fi(xi) subject to Σ xi = S. That is a knapsack, solved exactly by

D[i+1][s+x] = max( D[i+1][s+x], D[i][s] + fi(x) ),   answer D[n][S]

This takes n·S²/2 ≈ 50,000 steps for 10 castles and 100 soldiers. The streak rule (“3 in a row takes the rest”) breaks the castle-by-castle sum, so with it switched on the best reply is an approximation. Match scores always use the full rules.

The three live simulations

  • Tournament: a FiveThirtyEight-“Riddler”-style round robin. Shows which simple ideas do well against a mixed field.
  • Evolution: a genetic algorithm. Fitness = win % in a round robin. Parents are picked by 3-way tournament selection. Crossover takes each castle from a random parent, then a repair step restores the total of S. Mutation moves a few soldiers between castles. Elites survive unchanged.
  • Fictitious play: repeatedly best-reply to the mixture of everything played so far. Brown and Robinson showed that in two-player zero-sum games the empirical mixture converges to a Nash equilibrium. Exploitability is the best reply’s edge, and it falls toward 0.
